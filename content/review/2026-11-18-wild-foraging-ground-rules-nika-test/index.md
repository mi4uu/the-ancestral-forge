A patch of mushrooms can look familiar. A cluster of berries can resemble something you’ve eaten before. Neither is enough to make a wild harvest safe. Identify what’s growing, assess the site and check the rules that apply there. If you can’t resolve an important question, leave it where it is.

Foraging involves three separate checks: what the organism is, whether the site is suitable, and whether you may collect there. Passing one does not answer the others. A correct identification does not tell you whether the site is suitable, and permission to collect does not make an unknown plant safe to eat.

## Safety

> **Not medical advice.** This article is for general information only. It does not diagnose or treat anything. Talk to a qualified health professional before using any herb or remedy, especially if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, on medication or have a medical condition.

Some wild mushrooms are deadly. Poison Control warns that symptoms can be delayed for many hours, and some mushrooms can damage the liver, sometimes so severely that a transplant is needed. Don’t eat a wild mushroom unless an expert has identified it.

Pokeweed is poisonous throughout. Poison Control says eating several berries can cause stomach pain, nausea, vomiting and diarrhoea. Eating the root has caused bloody vomiting, bloody diarrhoea and low blood pressure. Pokeberries can hang in grape-like clusters, and pokeweed’s purple-red stems can be a clue to its identity.

If you’re in Australia and suspect poisoning, call the Poisons Information Centre on **13 11 26**. Call **000** in an emergency. These Australian numbers are distinct from the US Poison Control guidance cited below.

## Identify Before You Harvest

A casual visual match is not a positive identification. Neither is a single feature, such as a berry’s colour or a mushroom’s cap shape. Poison Control advises against eating wild mushrooms unless an expert identifies them. Poisonous species can resemble ones a picker recognises, and some toxins cause delayed illness.

If you can’t establish what a plant or mushroom is, don’t collect it for food. A familiar name, a photograph or a plant you saw elsewhere does not settle the question. For mushrooms, expert identification is the standard to follow before eating.

## Build a Mushroom Identification

A mushroom’s appearance is a collection of features, not a single silhouette. Poison Control says identification can involve the cap, gills or pores, spore colour, stalk and its base, changes caused by bruising or cutting, growth pattern, and what the mushroom grows on. A responsible identification considers several features together.

A general photo may omit the underside, the base of the stalk or the material the mushroom grows on. Poison Control also cautions that knowledge from another region may not reliably identify local mushrooms. Lookalikes can fool even people who feel confident, so seek expertise familiar with the mushrooms in your area.

These features can help explain what an expert considers; they don’t prove a mushroom is edible when checked on their own. If an expert can’t identify it with confidence, don’t eat it.

## Recognise Plant Lookalikes

Poison Control describes pokeweed berries as hanging in grape-like clusters and notes the plant’s purple-red stems. Pokeweed roots have also been mistaken for ginseng. The lookalikes illustrate why one suggestive clue or resemblance cannot establish that a wild plant is safe to eat.

Don’t taste an unknown plant to identify it, and don’t assume that an edible reputation applies to a similar-looking species. When you can’t confirm the identity, leave it in place and seek qualified local guidance.

## Don’t Rely on Cooking

Cooking does not make a poisonous mushroom safe. Poison Control explicitly rejects the idea that heat removes the danger. Cooking fumes from some poisonous mushrooms can also cause poisoning, so don’t put a questionable mushroom in a pan to test it.

Pokeweed is poisonous throughout, according to Poison Control. Information about particular plant parts does not provide a general preparation method or make uncertain identification acceptable. Don’t try to make an unknown wild plant safe with heat.

## Assess the Site

Correctly identifying a species doesn’t tell you whether the place it grew is suitable for harvesting. Consider the site before collecting, and avoid a place where you have a specific reason to suspect contamination unless reliable local guidance resolves the concern. Washing does not establish that produce from a polluted or chemically treated site is safe.

Keep the site check separate from identification and permission. A clean-looking patch does not make an unknown plant safe, and correct identification does not settle whether a site is suitable.

## Check Land Rules Before Collecting

Collection rules depend on the land and the authority responsible for it. Check the current rules for the specific site before harvesting. Permission on one parcel, park or forest does not establish permission elsewhere.

Shenandoah National Park in the United States provides a site-specific example. The National Park Service says plants there are generally protected from disturbance or collection. The park lists exceptions for small amounts of specified fruit and nuts for personal use, generally up to one quart per person per day. That allowance applies to Shenandoah’s listed exceptions, not to other sites.

The park also allows small amounts of edible mushrooms for personal use, while restricting collection of medicinal, woody shelf and other inedible mushrooms. Its page says mushrooms should not be eaten unless correctly identified as edible. Read the rules for the land you are on, including any limits on what may be collected and for what purpose. If they’re unclear, confirm them with the relevant authority.

## If Someone Eats a Suspect Mushroom

After suspected wild-mushroom ingestion in Australia, call the Poisons Information Centre on **13 11 26** promptly. Don’t wait for symptoms: poisoning may take many hours to become apparent. Call **000** in an emergency and follow the professionals’ advice.

The Poison Control guidance cited here is from the United States. In Australia, use the local contact details above. For any foraging trip, identify before harvesting, assess the site and check the rules. If you can’t resolve a key question, don’t collect it for food.
