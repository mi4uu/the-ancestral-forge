## The Lost History

*Lemna minor* has been consumed by humans for at least two thousand years. In Traditional Chinese Medicine, it is called *Fu Ping* (浮萍) and appears in the *Shennong Bencao Jing* (c. 200 CE) as a treatment for edema, skin eruptions, and febrile diseases. The herb was dried, decocted, or applied as a paste. Ancient Chinese farmers also fed it to ducks—hence the common name “duckweed.”

In Southeast Asia, rural communities in Vietnam, Thailand, and Myanmar have long gathered *Lemna* for soups and salads. In India, Ayurvedic texts describe it as *Shaliparni* (though the taxon is sometimes confused) and use it for urinary and respiratory conditions. The plant requires no cultivation—only a basket to skim it from the water.

During the 1940s food shortages in Japan, *Lemna* was promoted as a emergency protein source. Post‑war industrial agriculture killed that knowledge. Today, the West is rediscovering it through expensive “superfood” brands that sell dried duckweed powder for $30 per pound. That is a trap. You can grow a pound of *Lemna minor* for the cost of a bucket and a few gallons of nutrient‑rich water.

## The Protocol: Off‑Grid *Lemna minor* Production

### Step 1: Source Water & Nutrients

Do not collect from polluted ponds unless you have heavy‑metal testing equipment. Ideal: a 50‑gallon food‑grade plastic tank or a lined shallow pond (1 x 2 meters, 20 cm deep). Fill with non‑chlorinated water. Add nitrogen and phosphorus:

- **Urine** (diluted 10:1 with water) is ideal. Provides N, P, K, and micronutrients.
- **Alternatively:** 1 tablespoon of urea + 1 teaspoon of diammonium phosphate per 100 liters of water, added weekly.

Maintain pH 6.5–7.5. Test with strips.

### Step 2: Inoculation

Obtain a starter culture from a clean natural pond, from a fellow off‑grid practitioner, or from a biological supply house (e.g., Carolina Biological). Add ~100 g fresh biomass per square meter of surface. Keep water temperature 20–30°C. Shade from direct midday sun if water heats above 35°C.

### Step 3: Harvesting

After 7–10 days, the surface will be covered with a dense mat. Harvest every 2–3 days by skimming with a fine mesh net or a kitchen sieve. Collect up to 60% of the mat. Leave the rest to regrow.

**Yield:** From a 1‑square‑meter surface, you can harvest 200–400 g fresh biomass every 2–3 days. That's 70–140 g dry matter (35% protein = 25–50 g protein per harvest).

### Step 4: Processing for Human Consumption

**Washing:** Rinse three times in clean water (or boil for 1 minute to eliminate pathogens).

**Drying:** Spread in a thin layer (≤ 5 mm) on a mesh screen in full sun or in a dehydrator at 50°C for 6–8 hours. The dry material should snap when bent.

**Storage:** Store as whole dried flakes or grind to a fine powder in a coffee mill. Keep in an airtight jar in a cool, dark place. Shelf life: 12 months if moisture < 8%.

**Consumption:** Add 1–2 tablespoons of powder to smoothies, soups, or stews. Start with 10 g/day and increase to 30 g/day over two weeks. Blanching (1 minute in boiling water) reduces oxalate content by 40%.

### Safety Warnings

- Do not use biomass grown on industrial wastewater, gray water containing cleaning chemicals, or water from agricultural runoff with known pesticide use.
- Do not exceed 50 g dry matter per day without medical supervision. Oxalate content (0.5–1.5 g/100g dry) can cause kidney stone risk in predisposed individuals.
- Allergic reactions are rare but possible. Discontinue if rash or gastrointestinal distress occurs.
