A few pots can supply a home tea garden without taking over a whole bed. Lemon balm brings aromatic leaves, while mint needs room of its own and chamomile adds flowers to the planting. Plan for the harvest before you start cutting: lemon balm and mint hold plenty of moisture, so they need prompt drying and good airflow.

## Plan a small tea garden

Start with a small planting and choose herbs suited to your site. Penn State Extension recommends checking plant labels and botanical names, considering hardiness and growing conditions, and getting a soil test before planting. Herbs can grow in containers as well as in garden beds.

Lemon balm (*Melissa officinalis*) is a perennial that can grow up to 3 feet tall and is hardy to Zone 5. Penn State’s lemon-balm guidance says it prefers well-drained soil with a pH of 5–7.5, and can thrive in a large container. Choose a spot where water won’t linger around the roots.

Mint spreads through creeping stems. Penn State Extension recommends containing it so it doesn’t take over neighbouring plots. One option is a 3 to 5 gallon pot with drainage holes, sunk into the ground with its rim about an inch above the soil. Prune creeping stems that escape the pot. Mint can also get rust; watch the plants as they grow. Pinching mint encourages fuller growth.

For a small garden, pots make it easier to give mint its own space. Keep its label with the pot, and note the botanical name when you buy it. Chamomile also belongs in the garden only when you can identify the plant by a reliable label and botanical name. Penn State advises checking names because plants with similar common names can differ in their properties and uses.

Perennial herbs need attention as seasons change. Penn State recommends mulching perennials about 4 inches deep in winter. A modest garden is easier to tend when paths and containers are accessible, and when each plant has a clear place.

## Grow and keep track of the herbs

Lemon balm can spread if left unattended. Plant it in a large container or a contained area, and check its growth through the season. A pot gives it room while keeping the planting bounded. Mint needs a more deliberate barrier: use a container with drainage, keep the rim above soil, and prune stems that creep beyond it.

Labels matter both in the garden and after harvest. Write down each herb’s botanical name when you plant it, then keep the name with the harvest. Once leaves are detached and dried, many herbs look alike. Oregon State University Extension recommends labelling herbs at harvest.

Chamomile’s place here is as a correctly identified garden herb, not as a plant with a growing or harvest schedule supported by the sources in this article. Choose a named plant and follow its own label and local growing guidance. Don’t assume a common name alone settles what you have.

## Harvest leaves and flowers

For the leaf herbs, lemon balm and mint, harvest for drying just before the flowers open. Oregon State University Extension and the National Center for Home Food Preservation recommend harvesting after morning dew has evaporated. Penn State notes that leaves harvested then hold more essential oils.

The flower-stage advice applies to these leaf herbs, not to chamomile. Chamomile’s flowers are the part used, and the sources here don’t give guidance on when to harvest them. Follow reliable guidance for the identified plant rather than applying the leaf-harvest timing to its flowers.

Inspect each harvest. Remove bruised, soiled or imperfect material, and check for insects. Handle leaves gently to limit bruising. Penn State recommends leaving 4–6 inches (10–15 cm) of stem when cutting flowering herbs for regrowth.

Oregon State and the National Center for Home Food Preservation recommend rinsing herbs with cool water. Remove excess moisture after rinsing. Lemon balm and mint have high moisture content and can mould if they stay damp or dry too slowly, so don’t leave them in wet piles while you prepare the drying space.

## Dry the herbs promptly

Spread leaves so they don’t touch, or make small bunches with good airflow around them. Keep dust off the herbs while allowing air to circulate. Avoid crowded piles: lemon balm and mint need to dry promptly.

A dehydrator gives a controlled option. Penn State recommends 95–110°F (35–43°C). Oregon State recommends 95–115°F (35–46°C), and the National Center for Home Food Preservation gives the same range. A setting of 95 to 110°F (35 to 43°C) sits inside all three sources’ ranges. Oregon State and the National Center for Home Food Preservation allow temperatures up to 125°F in humid conditions.

Oregon State says dehydrator drying may take 1 to 4 hours. Check the herbs rather than relying on the clock; batch size and conditions affect the time. For air-drying leaves on a tray, Oregon State gives a much longer estimate: 5 to 10 days. Keep the leaves spread out and protected from dust as they dry.

## Check dryness and store

Leaves should be crisp and easy to crumble, and stems should snap when bent. Oregon State and the National Center for Home Food Preservation give these as signs that herbs are dry. Penn State also advises making sure the leaves are completely dry before storage.

Once dry, put the herbs in airtight containers and store them in a cool, dry, dark place. Keep the container labelled with the herb name. Oregon State says dried herbs retain their best flavour for six months to one year, so rotate older jars forward as you add a new harvest.

Penn State describes heat treatment for insects in air-dried herbs and seeds: heating at 160°F (71°C) for 30 minutes. It notes that heat reduces flavour. Its guidance also describes freezing at 0°F (−17°C) or lower for at least 48 hours. Consider the flavour trade-off when choosing a method.

## Prepare a culinary infusion

Penn State describes lemon-balm leaves as a way to flavour teas and infusions. Mint and chamomile can be part of a home tea garden, too. Use herbs for enjoyment, and follow a reliable culinary preparation for the cup you want; the sources cited here don’t give a recipe or proportions for combining these three herbs.

This is a drink to enjoy, and herbs aren’t a substitute for medical care. A familiar culinary use doesn’t establish a health effect. For health questions, speak with a qualified professional.

## Safety

Inspect herbs for insects, and discard soiled or damaged leaves. Rinse with cool water, remove excess moisture and dry promptly with good airflow. Seal herbs only when the leaves are crisp and stems snap. Damp herbs stored in an airtight container can mould.

Check the identity of any plant before you cut it, and never use one you can’t name. Keep botanical names and labels with the plants and harvest. After drying, many herbs look alike, so label containers before putting them away.
