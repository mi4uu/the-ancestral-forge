# Rhizome of Immortality: Protocol for Preparing Extracts from Angelica keiskei at Home

## Abstract

The modern pharmaceutical industry sells isolated molecules in plastic capsules, while the most potent bioactive compounds still grow in the soil. *Angelica keiskei*, known as Ashitaba, contains high concentrations of chalcones—primarily xanthoangelol and 4-hydroxyderricin—capable of modulating oxidative stress, activating autophagy pathways, and protecting mitochondrial function.

This plant was used by the inhabitants of the Izu Islands as a daily regenerative food long before laboratories began patenting the concept of "longevity."

This document presents a complete protocol for preparing water and alcohol extracts at home: from raw material collection, through drying parameters, to extraction ratios and stabilization of the finished preparation.

# 1. Introduction

## Hook: Modern humans have lost contact with their own pharmacy

Today's human can order a supplement via an app but cannot recognize a plant capable of protecting their nervous system from oxidative degradation.

This is not progress. This is outsourcing biological independence.

*Angelica keiskei Koidzumi*, a perennial plant from the Apiaceae family, was used for centuries by Japanese fishermen and farmers as a convalescent food. Its name "Ashitaba" literally means "leaf of tomorrow"—because after cutting the leaves, new growth appears within 24 hours.

This remarkable regenerative ability is not a poetic metaphor. It is a consequence of intense production of protective secondary metabolites, primarily chalcones.

Unlike most modern supplements, Ashitaba does not act through a single mechanism. It acts as a comprehensive regulator of cellular stress:

- limits lipid peroxidation,
- modulates inflammatory cytokines,
- supports endothelial function,
- activates autophagy processes,
- increases mitochondrial resistance to metabolic stress.

This is not a "superfood." It is a biological survival tool.

# 2. Background

## Lost history: The Izu Islands and the plant of resilience

Ashitaba occurs naturally on the volcanic islands of southern Japan: Hachijō-jima, Mikura-jima, and Ōshima. Mineral-rich soils and constant exposure to harsh maritime conditions forced the plant to develop an exceptionally strong defense system based on chalcones.

Local inhabitants consumed fresh leaves, dried them for winter, and prepared infusions. Epidemiological studies conducted in the 20th century showed that the population of these islands exhibited a significantly lower incidence of age-related diseases compared to the national average.

This phenomenon was not attributed to genetics but to diet. Ashitaba was a staple, not a supplement.

Modern science has confirmed what folk medicine knew: chalcones from *Angelica keiskei* activate the Nrf2 transcription factor, which is the master regulator of the antioxidant response. They also inhibit the NF-κB pathway, reducing chronic inflammation—the foundation of aging and degenerative diseases.

# 3. Materials and Methods

## Raw material: Fresh or dried leaves and stems

The optimal source is fresh Ashitaba leaves, preferably from organic cultivation. The plant is biennial; the highest concentration of chalcones occurs in the first year of growth, before flowering. Leaves should be harvested in the morning, after dew has evaporated, but before intense sunlight.

If fresh material is unavailable, high-quality dried leaves from trusted suppliers can be used. However, drying reduces chalcone content by 15–30%, depending on conditions.

### Drying protocol:

- Temperature: 35–40°C (95–104°F) – never exceed 45°C, as chalcones degrade.
- Method: air drying in a dark, ventilated room or a dehydrator with precise temperature control.
- Time: 24–48 hours until leaves are brittle.
- Storage: in airtight glass jars, away from light and moisture, for up to 12 months.

## Extraction solvents

Two types of extracts are recommended:

1. **Alcohol extract (tincture):** Ethanol 40–60% (vodka or food-grade ethanol diluted with distilled water). Ethanol extracts chalcones, flavonoids, and essential oils.
2. **Water extract (infusion/decoction):** Distilled water. Water extracts polysaccharides, some flavonoids, and water-soluble vitamins.

## Equipment

- Glass jars with tight lids (for maceration)
- Dark glass bottles for storage
- Fine mesh strainer or cheesecloth
- Coffee filter or paper filter
- Kitchen scale (precision 0.1 g)
- Thermometer (for water extraction)
- Dehydrator or drying rack (optional)

# 4. Protocol: Alcohol Extract (Tincture)

## Step 1: Preparation of plant material

Use fresh or dried leaves. If fresh, chop finely to increase surface area. For dried, grind to a coarse powder (not fine dust, as filtration becomes difficult).

## Step 2: Ratio

Standard ratio: 1 part plant material to 5 parts solvent (by weight). For fresh leaves, use 1:5 (e.g., 100 g fresh leaves to 500 ml solvent). For dried, use 1:10 (e.g., 50 g dried to 500 ml solvent) because dried material is more concentrated.

## Step 3: Maceration

Place plant material in a glass jar. Pour the alcohol solvent over it, ensuring complete coverage. Close the jar tightly and shake well. Store in a dark place at room temperature (20–25°C) for 4–6 weeks. Shake daily for the first week, then once a week.

## Step 4: Filtration

After maceration, strain through a fine mesh strainer or cheesecloth. Then filter through a coffee filter to remove fine particles. Press the marc (solid residue) to extract remaining liquid.

## Step 5: Storage

Pour the tincture into dark glass bottles. Label with date, concentration, and plant part. Store in a cool, dark place. Shelf life: 2–3 years.

## Dosage

Typical dosage: 1–3 ml (20–60 drops) 2–3 times daily, diluted in water or juice. Start with a low dose to assess tolerance.

# 5. Protocol: Water Extract (Infusion/Decoction)

## Step 1: Preparation

Use dried leaves (preferred for water extract to avoid microbial growth). Crush lightly.

## Step 2: Ratio

1 tablespoon (approx. 5 g) per 250 ml water.

## Step 3: Extraction

Bring water to a boil, then remove from heat. Add plant material, cover, and steep for 15–20 minutes. For a stronger decoction, simmer gently for 10 minutes, then steep.

## Step 4: Filtration

Strain through a fine sieve or cheesecloth. Do not press the leaves, as this releases bitter compounds.

## Step 5: Storage

Water extracts are perishable. Store in the refrigerator for up to 48 hours. For longer storage, freeze in ice cube trays.

## Dosage

Drink 1–2 cups daily, preferably between meals.

# 6. Quality Control and Stability

## Visual inspection

Fresh tincture should be dark green to brown. Water extract is light green to amber. Cloudiness or mold indicates contamination.

## Organoleptic properties

Ashitaba has a characteristic bitter, slightly celery-like taste. A strong bitter taste indicates high chalcone content.

## Stability

Chalcones are sensitive to light, heat, and oxygen. Store all extracts in dark glass, away from heat sources. Tinctures are more stable than water extracts.

## Testing for chalcones (simple home method)

Add a few drops of tincture to a small amount of 10% sodium hydroxide solution (lye water). A yellow to orange color indicates the presence of chalcones (due to the formation of chalcone salts). This is a qualitative test, not quantitative.

# 7. Discussion

## Why home extraction?

Commercial supplements often contain isolated chalcones or standardized extracts, but the full spectrum of compounds in the whole plant may offer synergistic effects. Home extraction allows control over quality, avoids additives, and reduces cost.

## Limitations

- Variability in plant material: chalcone content depends on growing conditions, harvest time, and drying.
- Lack of standardization: home extracts cannot guarantee a specific concentration of active compounds.
- Risk of contamination: improper drying or storage can lead to mold or bacterial growth.

## Safety

*Angelica keiskei* is generally recognized as safe when used as food. However, due to its coumarin content (though low in this species), individuals taking anticoagulant medications should consult a healthcare provider. Pregnant or nursing women should avoid high doses.
