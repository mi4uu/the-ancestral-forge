# Backyard Protein: How to Integrate Insects and Small Animals into a Closed-Loop Garden System

## Abstract

Modern food systems transport protein thousands of kilometers, consume enormous quantities of nitrogen fertilizers, and convert fertile soil into monoculture feed production. This is a structure dependent on diesel, refrigeration, and stable supply chains. One energy crisis is sufficient to turn supermarket meat into a luxury.

Yet there is an older model of production. Fly larvae decompose kitchen waste. Rabbits convert weeds and leaves into meat and nitrogen-rich fertilizer. Quail produce eggs on an area smaller than a workbench. Everything returns to the soil. Nothing is wasted.

This is not a hobby. It is a biological infrastructure of resilience.

---

# 1. Introduction

## The Hook

Most people do not understand a fundamental biological fact: a vegetable garden without a source of nitrogen and protein is an incomplete system.

Plants extract minerals from the soil. Humans consume the harvest. Waste is removed. Nitrogen disappears from the cycle. Then dependence arises on bagged synthetic fertilizer produced by the Haber–Bosch method at temperatures exceeding 400 °C and pressures around 200 atmospheres.

Our ancestors solved this problem differently. Every farm was a closed metabolic system. Animals, insects, compost, and crops formed a single organism.

Today, the average home garden produces calories. It rarely produces complete protein, fat, and fertilizer simultaneously.

This is a design flaw.

---

## The Lost History

In medieval Europe, rabbits were a standard element of small farms because they could convert fibrous biomass into meat with a feed conversion ratio significantly better than that of cattle. In Southeast Asia, larvae of *Hermetia illucens* — the black soldier fly — naturally colonized organic waste, turning rotting matter into concentrated protein and fat.

Even earlier, the peoples of Mesoamerica collected eggs of aquatic bugs from Lake Texcoco as a source of amino acids and micronutrients. The Romans fattened dormice. China developed integrated aquaculture and duck husbandry systems over a thousand years ago.

The food industry displaced these solutions not because they were ineffective. It displaced them because they could not be easily patented or centralized.
