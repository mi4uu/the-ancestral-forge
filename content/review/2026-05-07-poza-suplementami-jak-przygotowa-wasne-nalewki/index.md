## 2. The Lost History

Long before the first supplement factory was built, tinctures were the backbone of Western and Eastern medicine. The word “tincture” derives from the Latin *tinctura*, meaning “a dyeing” or “a soaking.” The process is simple: submerge plant material in a high-proof alcohol, let it sit for a period of time, and then strain. Alcohol is a universal solvent—it draws out alkaloids, resins, volatile oils, and glycosides that water alone cannot touch.

The first recorded use of alcohol-based herbal extracts comes from the ancient Greeks. Hippocrates, writing in the 4th century BCE, described the use of wine as a solvent for extracting the medicinal properties of wormwood and other bitter herbs. The Roman physician Dioscorides, in his *De Materia Medica* (c. 60 CE), catalogued over 600 plants and detailed the preparation of “wine infusions” that were essentially tinctures. By the Middle Ages, European monasteries had perfected the art of *aqua vitae*—water of life—made by distilling herbs in alcohol. The apothecaries of the 17th and 18th centuries relied almost exclusively on tinctures, and the famous *Pharmacopoeia Londinensis* (1618) included precise recipes for dozens of them.

The industrial revolution and the rise of synthetic pharmaceuticals pushed tinctures to the margins. Pills and tablets were easier to mass-produce and patent. But the wisdom of the old apothecaries remains valid: alcohol not only extracts, it preserves. A properly made tincture can maintain potency for decades, whereas dried herbs lose up to 50 percent of their active compounds within a year.

The commercial supplement industry, obsessed with shelf life and convenience, opted for powders and capsules. They ignored the fundamental fact that the human body evolved to process plant compounds in a liquid, partially digested state—not encapsulated in a dry, fibrous matrix. The result is a market flooded with products that are, at best, weakly effective. The time has come to reclaim the old knowledge.

## 3. The Science / Math

### 3.1 Bioavailability: Why Alcohol Works

Bioavailability is the fraction of an ingested substance that reaches the systemic circulation in an active form. For oral supplements, bioavailability is limited by three factors: release from the matrix, solubility in the gastrointestinal fluids, and metabolism by the liver and gut microbes (first-pass effect). Dry plant material, especially when powdered, has a low release rate because the cell walls remain intact. The body must physically break down the cellulose, a process that is slow and incomplete.

Alcohol, by contrast, denatures cell membranes and dissolves both polar and non-polar compounds. Ethanol concentrations between 40% and 70% (80 to 140 proof) are optimal: they extract water-soluble glycosides and alkaloids, as well as alcohol-soluble resins and essential oils. Once ingested, the alcohol carries these compounds directly into the bloodstream via the oral mucosa (sublingual absorption) and the stomach lining, bypassing the liver’s first-pass metabolism. This is why sublingual tincture administration can achieve bioavailability rates of 50–80% for many compounds, compared to less than 10% for oral capsules.

### 3.2 The Chemistry of Extraction

The key to a potent tincture is the polarity of the solvent. Water is a polar solvent; it extracts sugars, amino acids, and some glycosides. Ethanol is less polar and extracts non-polar compounds like alkaloids, essential oils, and resins. A mixture of ethanol and water (hydroethanolic solution) creates a gradient of polarity that can extract the widest range of phytochemicals.

For example, the active compound in *Echinacea purpurea*—the alkylamide—is best extracted at 60% ethanol. The bitter compounds in *Gentiana lutea* (gentiopicroside) are efficiently extracted at 40% ethanol. The alkaloids in *Hydrastis canadensis* (berberine) require 70% ethanol. This is not guesswork; it is a function of the partition coefficient (log P) of each compound. A good tincture uses a solvent that matches the target compounds.

### 3.3 Yields and Concentrations

A standard tincture uses a 1:5 ratio of dried plant material to solvent (100 grams of dried herb to 500 mL of solvent). For fresh plant material, the ratio is 1:2 (200 grams fresh herb to 400 mL of solvent). The alcohol content is adjusted to account for the water content of fresh plants. The resulting tincture contains approximately 200–400 mg of active compounds per milliliter, depending on the plant. Compare this to a typical 500 mg capsule of dried herb, which may contain only 50 mg of actual active compounds after digestion—and you are ingesting the capsule filler, binder, and flow agent as well.

The math is unambiguous: a properly made tincture delivers 5 to 10 times the effective dose of a commercial supplement, with superior absorption. The cost per dose is also a fraction of the retail price. A bottle of 500 mL of 40% ethanol (vodka) costs about $10. Dried *Echinacea* root costs $15 per pound. The resulting tincture yields over 200 doses, each costing less than $0.15. The equivalent commercial supplement would cost $30 for 60 capsules.

## 4. The Protocol

### 4.1 Materials and Equipment

- **Alcohol:** 80–100 proof vodka (40–50% ethanol) or food-grade 190 proof grain alcohol (95% ethanol) diluted to 60–70% with distilled water. For dried herbs, 40–50% is sufficient. For fresh herbs, use 60–70% to compensate for water content.
- **Plant material:** Fresh or dried, organically grown, properly identified. Use the part specified in medicinal tradition (root, leaf, flower, bark). Always dry fresh material lightly to reduce moisture.
- **Glass jar:** Wide-mouth Mason jar, sterilized. Size depends on batch: 1 quart for up to 200g dried herb.
- **Scale:** Precision to 1 gram.
- **Strainer:** Fine mesh or cheesecloth.
- **Dark glass bottles:** Amber or cobalt blue with dropper caps.
- **Labels:** Permanent marker or pre-printed labels.

### 4.2 Step-by-Step Instructions

**Step 1: Prepare the plant material.**  
If using dried herbs, chop or grind them coarsely. Do not powder them—fine particles will clog the strainer. For fresh herbs, chop into small pieces (1 cm). Measure the weight. For dried, use 100 grams per 500 mL of alcohol. For fresh, use 200 grams per 500 mL of alcohol.

**Step 2: Combine plant and alcohol.**  
Place the plant material in the glass jar. Pour the alcohol over it, ensuring the material is completely submerged. Seal the jar tightly. Shake vigorously for 30 seconds.

**Step 3: Macerate.**  
Store the jar in a cool, dark place (60–70°F, 15–21°C). Shake the jar once daily for the first week, then once every three days. The maceration time depends on the plant material:
- Soft leaves and flowers: 2 weeks
- Roots and bark: 4 weeks
- Fresh plants: 4–6 weeks

**Step 4: Strain.**  
After maceration, pour the mixture through a fine mesh strainer or cheesecloth into a clean bowl. Squeeze the remaining plant material to extract as much liquid as possible. Discard the marc (pressed plant matter).

**Step 5: Bottle and store.**  
Pour the tincture into amber glass bottles using a funnel. Label with the plant name, alcohol percentage, date of preparation, and dosage. Store in a dark cabinet at room temperature. Tinctures last for 5 years or more.

### 4.3 Dosage and Administration

Standard dosage is 1–2 mL (30–60 drops) taken 2–3 times daily. For acute conditions, 3–5 mL can be used for up to 3 days. Administer sublingually: hold under the tongue for 30 seconds before swallowing, to maximize absorption through the oral mucosa. Always start with a low dose to test sensitivity.

### 4.4 Example: *Echinacea purpurea* Tincture for Immune Support

- **Plant material:** 100 g dried *Echinacea purpurea* root (authenticated)
- **Solvent:** 500 mL of 80 proof vodka (40% ethanol)
- **Maceration:** 4 weeks in dark, shaking every 3 days
- **Yield:** ~450 mL (some loss to plant material)
- **Dosage:** 2 mL (two dropperfuls) three times daily at onset of cold symptoms
- **Cost:** $0.15 per dose (vs. $0.50 for commercial equivalent)
