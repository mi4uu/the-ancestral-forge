# The Lie of Standardization: Why Powdered Herbs from the Supermarket Cannot Replace Fresh Raw Materials

## The Hook

The modern supplement industry has sold society convenience as an equivalent of quality. A capsule stamped with “500 mg standardized extract” is supposedly meant to replace a freshly harvested herbal raw material. This is a technological fiction built on marketing and a disregard for plant biology.

A plant is not a single molecule.

It is a living system of enzymes, volatile terpenes, glycosides, alkaloids, polyphenols, and cofactors acting within a dynamic biochemical matrix. At the moment of harvest, degradation begins. At the moment of grinding, it accelerates. At the moment of storage on a supermarket shelf, most of the most reactive fractions no longer exist.

Standardization does not signify compositional completeness. It signifies only that the manufacturer has measured one selected chemical marker and ignored the rest.

It is possible to produce a powder containing “5% curcuminoids” that is simultaneously biologically dead in terms of the activity of volatile sesquiterpenes, oxidoreductase enzymes, and unstable flavonoids.

This is not botanical medicine. This is an industrial reduction of a plant to a laboratory number.

# 1. Introduction

## The Problem of Industrial Standardization

The contemporary supplement market operates according to the logic of storage stability, not the biological integrity of the raw material.

Dried powders:
- withstand years of transport,
- tolerate high temperatures,
- can be machine-encapsulated,
- are inexpensive to standardize in the laboratory.

Fresh raw materials:
- oxidize rapidly,
- require immediate processing,
- have a variable chemical profile dependent on soil and season,
- are unsuitable for mass logistics.

Thus, the industry chose a product that is commercially stable, not biologically stable.

The most active plant fractions—monoterpenes, volatile phenols, oxidative enzymes, and part of the polyacetylenes—are simultaneously the least stable. They are lost during:
- convective drying,
- UV exposure,
- grinding,
- contact with oxygen,
- storage above 20°C.

As a result, the consumer receives a chemical shadow of the original plant.
