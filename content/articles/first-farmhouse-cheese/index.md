A pan of warm milk, a splash of vinegar or lemon juice, and a clean cloth can turn milk into soft curds and whey. University of Wyoming Extension and New Mexico State University describe this simple acid-set method. The visible change is easy to follow at the stove: solids gather, liquid separates, and the curds can be drained to the texture you want.

For a first batch, keep the aim modest. Choose suitable milk, separate the curds, drain them, season, then chill. The result is fresh cheese: moist, unripened and perishable.

## What makes a cheese “fresh”

Fresh cheese hasn’t gone through a ripening period. It’s soft or semi-soft, with moisture still in the curds. New Mexico State University’s guide to homemade cheese notes that soft cheeses contain more than 45 per cent water and are highly perishable. It gives soft cheeses a refrigerated life of only 5–7 days.

That short life is part of the style. The cheese is made, drained, chilled and eaten while fresh. Its simple method makes it approachable, but the cheese still needs careful handling.

Acid added to heated milk separates solid curds from liquid whey. Drain the curds and you have the basis of a fresh cheese. The texture depends in part on how much whey you let escape. University of Wyoming Extension describes longer draining as a way to make a firmer, drier cheese.

## The basic curds-and-whey method

Start by heating the milk, then add vinegar or lemon juice until the curds separate from the whey. Have your equipment and cloth ready before you begin, so you can handle the curds without searching for a clean utensil or surface.

Watch for the separation. University of Wyoming Extension says to heat until the curds separate. If they don’t, return the mixture to medium heat and add more acid. Once the curds have separated, transfer them to a clean cloth and let the whey drain. A longer drain produces a firmer, drier cheese; a shorter drain leaves it softer.

Salt or other flavourings can be added after draining. Work them through the curds with clean hands or a clean utensil. Seasoning changes the taste, while the texture comes from how the curds are drained.

## Choose milk that will set

University of Wyoming Extension says whole milk creates the best cheese. For a first attempt, choose pasteurised milk. Ultra-pasteurised milk may not set successfully, so a poor curd isn’t necessarily a sign that every step went wrong. Check the label and try a different pasteurised milk next time.

The CDC recommends choosing pasteurised milk and dairy products, and the FDA backs pasteurisation. Homemade cheese made from raw milk remains a raw-milk product unless the milk is properly pasteurised.

Pasteurisation and acid-setting have different roles in the method. Acid helps form curds; the appearance of curds doesn’t establish that the milk or finished cheese is free of pathogens. For a first batch, pasteurised milk keeps the process simple.

## Set up a clean work area

Wash your hands and clean equipment, cloths and food-contact surfaces before starting. New Mexico State University’s guide stresses clean equipment and surfaces, handwashing, and careful handling of curds. Once the milk has separated, the curds will be moved and drained, giving anything on hands or tools a chance to reach the food.

Clean the utensils and cloth you’ll use, along with the counter or other surface where equipment will rest. Keep clean items separate from anything that hasn’t been washed. A cloth that looks clean still needs cleaning before it touches food.

NMSU identifies *Salmonella*, *E. coli*, *Listeria monocytogenes* and *Staphylococcus aureus* among pathogens associated with fresh cheese. Contaminated hands, utensils, cloths or work surfaces can introduce germs during curd handling. Keep the work area orderly and avoid unnecessary handling.

## Drain, season and refrigerate

The cloth holds the curds while whey escapes. Drain longer for a firmer cheese; stop sooner for a softer result. Whatever texture you choose, the cheese remains moist and perishable.

Add salt or flavourings once the curds are drained enough to handle. Transfer the cheese to a clean container and refrigerate it promptly. The CDC says to keep perishable foods at 40°F (4°C) or colder. NMSU gives soft cheeses a refrigerated life of 5–7 days.

Serve what you need and return the rest to the refrigerator promptly. Keep track of when you made it, and discard it if it’s expired or spoiled.

## Safety

Raw milk can carry harmful germs, including *Campylobacter*, *E. coli* and *Listeria*. The CDC also lists *Cryptosporidium*, *Brucella* and *Salmonella* among germs that can be present in raw-milk products. The CDC says good practices on farms can reduce contamination, but can’t guarantee raw milk is free of harmful germs.

Children under 5, adults over 65, pregnant people and people with weakened immune systems are at higher risk of serious illness from germs in raw milk, according to the CDC. For a first batch, use pasteurised milk.

Keep the finished cheese cold. CDC guidance says to refrigerate perishables at 40°F (4°C) or colder. Don’t leave them out for more than 2 hours, or more than 1 hour when the temperature is above 90°F (32°C). When in doubt, throw it out.

Clean handling matters with pasteurised milk, too. NMSU recommends clean equipment and food-contact surfaces, proper cooking and cooling, careful curd handling and handwashing. If food has been left out beyond the CDC’s time limits, discard it.

## Troubleshooting a first batch

If the milk refuses to form clear curds, check what kind you bought. Ultra-pasteurised milk may not set successfully, University of Wyoming Extension notes. If the curds don’t separate during heating, return the mixture to medium heat and add more acid. Follow the method and keep your equipment clean.

A clear separation tells you the milk has curdled. It doesn’t tell you whether the finished cheese is free of pathogens. Choose pasteurised milk, handle the curds with clean equipment, and refrigerate the cheese promptly.

The process is a short sequence: choose suitable milk, heat and acidify it, drain cleanly, season, chill. Each step has its own job.

## Sharing or selling homemade cheese

Sharing cheese changes the question from kitchen method to local rules. The FDA prohibits interstate sales of raw milk, while rules for intrastate sale or distribution depend on state law. Requirements vary, so check the rules that apply where you live before selling or distributing homemade cheese.

That distinction matters if the cheese is made with raw milk. Don’t assume that a method allowed in one place is permitted in another, or that a homemade product is exempt from local requirements.
