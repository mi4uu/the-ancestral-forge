A pork belly rests in a bag in the refrigerator, its surface coated with salt, curing salt and seasonings. Five to seven days later, it should feel firm. There is still work to do: rinse it, cook or hot-smoke it to the recipe’s internal-temperature target, chill it, slice it, then cook the slices before eating.

Oregon State University Extension’s [Making cured bacon at home](https://extension.oregonstate.edu/catalog/pnw-784-making-cured-bacon-home) gives tested dry- and wet-cure recipes, along with instructions for curing and cooking. Use one of those recipes and check the curing-salt manufacturer’s directions. The ingredient amounts and steps belong together.

## What home-cured bacon involves

Bacon starts with pork belly. A dry cure coats the belly with a measured mixture of salt, curing salt and seasonings. A wet cure immerses it in brine made from those ingredients. Oregon State University Extension describes both as home-curing methods.

Curing is one stage in the process. After the refrigerator cure, OSU instructs home cooks to rinse the surface, pat the belly dry, then cook or hot-smoke it to an internal temperature of at least 160°F. Chill the cooked belly overnight. Slice it, and cook the slices before serving, as you would store-bought bacon.

That last cooking step is easy to miss. USDA’s HACCP model concerns commercial bacon receiving a heat treatment: that step does not necessarily make the bacon ready to eat. The model says it must be refrigerated or frozen and cooked before consumption. For home bacon, OSU’s instructions give the sequence: hot-smoke or cook to 160°F, chill, slice, then fry, bake or microwave the slices before eating.

## Dry cure or wet brine?

The distinction is how the measured cure is applied. For a dry cure, coat the pork belly with the recipe’s mixture. For a wet cure, immerse it in brine prepared from the specified ingredients. Choose the method in the tested recipe and follow its directions for the whole process.

OSU’s recipe examples cure the belly in a bag in the refrigerator for five to seven days, turning the bag daily. The meat should feel firm when ready. Keep to the recipe’s cure time and ingredient amounts; an extra day does not correct a change in the mixture.

USDA describes commercial immersion curing as placing bacon in brine for two to three days, then hanging it until cured. That is a description of commercial processing, not a home method to substitute for OSU’s recipes. Home cooks can start with the tested recipes on the OSU page, which explains both curing approaches and points readers to the curing-salt manufacturer’s instructions.

## Curing salt: what it does and how to use it

Nitrite inhibits *Clostridium botulinum*, contributes cured colour and flavour, and slows rancidity, Oregon State University Extension says. It has a defined role in the cure, and the recipe sets the amount.

OSU warns that curing salts are toxic at excessive amounts. Its example recipes use one teaspoon of Cure #1 for five pounds of pork belly and direct readers to check the curing-salt manufacturer’s instructions. Follow the specific recipe and product directions. Do not estimate the amount or change it.

Use the curing product named in the recipe. Ordinary salt and curing salt are different ingredients, and a recipe’s measured quantity is not an invitation to substitute another product or adjust the amount.

USDA’s stated nitrite limits for dry-cured bacon and its labelling rules apply to regulated commercial products. They are not home-batch instructions. USDA also explains that some ingredients used in products labelled “uncured” can naturally produce small amounts of nitrates. The label alone does not tell you how a home cure should be made.

## Cure time and refrigeration

For its home recipe examples, OSU gives a refrigerator cure of five to seven days, with the bag turned daily. The belly should feel firm when the cure is complete. Follow the recipe’s instructions for the ingredients, timing and refrigeration throughout.

OSU says bacon made without nitrite must be kept constantly below 40°F. It also says bacon can be made without nitrite. That storage instruction is specific to nitrite-free bacon; for either method, follow the chosen recipe’s refrigeration directions.

Nitrite helps inhibit botulism, but the recipe and cold storage still matter. The cure takes place in the refrigerator. Once the bacon is cooked, OSU advises chilling it overnight before slicing and storing it.

## Rinse, smoke and cook

After curing, rinse the surface of the belly and pat it dry, as OSU instructs. Rinsing reduces surface salt; it does not remove curing salts from the interior. It cannot correct an excess in the measured cure.

OSU’s example hot-smoking or cooking setting is 225–250°F. Its example takes about three hours to reach the target internal temperature, but the time depends on the piece. Use the internal-temperature target in the recipe rather than treating the estimated cooking time as the endpoint.

Cook or hot-smoke the belly to at least 160°F internally. Then chill it overnight, slice it, and cook the slices before serving. Fry, bake or microwave them as you would store-bought bacon. The first heat step and the later cooking of the slices are separate parts of OSU’s instructions.

## Cool and store

After the heat step, chill the bacon overnight. Oregon State University Extension advises refrigerating it for up to seven days or freezing it. For frozen bacon, OSU recommends use within four months for best quality.

Home-cured or smoked bacon should not be treated as shelf-stable on that basis alone. USDA explains that commercial shelf-stable bacon receives additional processing to control water activity. Curing or smoking at home does not establish that the bacon can be stored at room temperature.

Keep the finished bacon refrigerated or frozen and cook the slices before eating. For the storage period and freezing guidance, follow OSU’s instructions.

## Safety

The main hazards are excessive nitrite, botulism risk when nitrite-free bacon is stored too warm, and bacon that is treated as ready to eat when it still needs cooking. OSU warns that curing salts can be toxic at excessive amounts. Use the recipe’s measured quantity and the manufacturer’s directions.

For bacon made without nitrite, OSU calls for constant storage below 40°F. Nitrite inhibits *Clostridium botulinum*, but the nitrite-free option still needs its stated cold storage. Keep the cure refrigerated according to the recipe.

Nitrosamines can form from nitrites and amines under certain conditions, and cooking method and doneness influence their formation, USDA says. Oregon State University Extension advises avoiding well-done or burned bacon to reduce nitrosamine formation. Cook the slices before eating, and avoid cooking them until well-done or burned.

Finally, the heat step does not end the cooking instructions. Hot-smoke or cook the belly to at least 160°F, chill it overnight, slice it, then cook the slices before serving. Keep the finished bacon refrigerated or frozen.
