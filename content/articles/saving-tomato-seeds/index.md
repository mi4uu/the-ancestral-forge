A ripe tomato holds next season’s seed in a slippery gel. Separate that coating, dry the seeds thoroughly and label them well, and you can put a packet aside for planting. Two details deserve care: the variety you choose and the moisture left in the finished seed.

A saved seed is a small record of the fruit it came from. It may grow into a plant much like its parent, or it may not. Start with the right variety, pick ripe fruit, and keep each batch identified from the moment it leaves the tomato.

## Choose varieties worth saving

Start with the variety name, then check whether the tomato is open-pollinated. Oregon State University Extension Service and the University of California Agriculture and Natural Resources Cooperative Extension explain that open-pollinated varieties are a better choice when you want offspring to resemble the parent. Seed saved from an F1 hybrid may instead grow into plants with variable traits.

“Heirloom” does not answer the same question. The University of Vermont Extension and University of Minnesota Extension note that open-pollinated varieties can include newer selections as well as older ones. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources describes heirlooms as open-pollinated. For a particular variety, check its stated status rather than relying on the label alone.

If you want to repeat a tomato’s qualities in a future garden, choose an open-pollinated variety and keep its identity clear. You can still save seed from an F1 hybrid, but the next generation may not resemble the fruit you chose. Make a note of the variety when you plant it. A clear record now saves guesswork when you come to collect seed.

## Pick ripe, healthy fruit

Choose healthy, fully ripe tomatoes for seed. Oregon State University Extension Service, UC Agriculture and Natural Resources, and the University of Vermont Extension all recommend ripe, healthy fruit as the starting point. Pick more than one tomato if you can. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources recommends saving seed from several fruits and, if possible, more than one plant to maintain genetic diversity.

Keep the variety in mind as you select. Tomato flowers generally self-fertilise, but bees can cross-pollinate them. If different varieties are growing nearby, take note. For seed that is more likely to produce plants like the parent, favour open-pollinated varieties and consider whether cross-pollination may have occurred.

Keep each variety separate as you gather fruit. Mark the batch before bringing it inside, while you still know where it came from.

## Scoop out the seeds and gel

Cut or open a ripe tomato, then scoop or squeeze the seeds and their surrounding gel into a container. Oregon State University Extension Service, UC Agriculture and Natural Resources, and the University of Vermont Extension describe this wet method for collecting tomato seed.

Fermentation is one way to separate the gel from the seeds. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources says it is optional, and describes it as a way to make seeds easier to separate from gel. Use a separate container for each variety and label it before setting it aside. Record the variety and date.

The purpose is to clean seed for planting. After cleaning, tip out the seed-and-gel mixture. Don’t eat it.

## Ferment the seed mixture

Oregon State University Extension Service recommends fermenting the seed-and-gel mixture for about 72 hours at 72–80°F. University of Minnesota Extension says to swirl it twice daily and notes that seeds should sink within five days. Keep track of when the batch began and follow the guidance you’re using.

UC Agriculture and Natural Resources considers fermentation optional. It says fermentation can help separate seeds from gel, sort out poor seeds, reduce some seed-borne illnesses and remove a germination inhibitor. If you choose to ferment, leave the labelled container where you can return to it and swirl it twice each day, as Minnesota Extension instructs.

When you’re ready to clean the seeds, pour off the liquid and rinse them. The mixture is for seed cleaning only, not a food-preservation ferment.

## Rinse and separate the seeds

After fermentation, pour off the liquid and rinse the seeds with water. University of Minnesota Extension and Oregon State University Extension Service both describe rinsing as the next step. Work over a strainer or suitable container so the small seeds don’t go down the drain. Rinse and gently move them until the surrounding material has been removed.

UC Agriculture and Natural Resources says fermentation can help separate seeds from gel and remove a germination inhibitor. The immediate task is to remove the surrounding material before drying.

Spread the rinsed seeds on a drying surface. Keep the batch with its label so the variety stays identified through the next steps.

## Dry seeds thoroughly

Spread the rinsed seeds in a thin layer, with good air circulation. Oregon State University Extension Service recommends good airflow. The University of Vermont Extension says drying may take one to three weeks, or longer in humid conditions. Seeds need to be thoroughly dry before storage.

Avoid piling seeds in a heap. Keep them somewhere they can dry undisturbed, and allow more time if the air is humid. Heat is not a shortcut. Oregon State warns that seed damage begins above 95°F, so keep drying temperatures below that point.

The University of Vermont Extension warns that seed that is not thoroughly dry can mould in storage, affecting other seeds in the same container. Leave the batch to dry longer before packing it away if it remains damp.

## Label and store the seed

When the seed is dry, pack it in a labelled packet. Write the variety and date, as Oregon State University Extension Service and the University of Vermont Extension advise. Store packets in a cool, dark, dry place. Oregon State and Vermont Extension say a sealed container can help protect seed from pests and excess moisture.

If you refrigerate or freeze seed, let the closed container return to room temperature before opening it. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources recommends this to reduce condensation moisture entering the jar.

## Hybrids, crosses and seed purity

Tomato flowers generally self-fertilise, but bees can carry pollen between varieties. UC Agriculture and Natural Resources notes that this can create crosses. Seed from an open-pollinated tomato may therefore fail to remain true to type if it crossed with a nearby variety.

If preserving varietal purity matters, UC Agriculture and Natural Resources suggests bagging blossoms. It also recommends saving seed from several fruits and, if possible, more than one plant to maintain genetic diversity. Keep the saved batch tied to its variety through collection, cleaning and storage.

The hybrid question is separate from cross-pollination. Seed saved from an F1 hybrid may produce variable plants because the next generation can differ from the parent. A cross between varieties can also change what grows from seed, even when the parent itself was open-pollinated.

For seed likely to resemble the parent, open-pollinated status is the useful guide. “Heirloom” may describe a variety, but check its status rather than relying on that word alone.

## Safety

Damp seed can mould and contaminate other seed stored in the same container. The University of Vermont Extension warns that seed must be thoroughly dry before storage. Leave it out to dry longer before sealing it away.

Next, watch the heat. Oregon State University Extension Service warns that seed damage begins above 95°F. Keep the drying temperature below that point.

Finally, the fermenting seed-and-gel mixture is for seed cleaning only. It is not a food-preservation process. Don’t eat it; tip it out once the seed is clean.
