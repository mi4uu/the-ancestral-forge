A drop in temperature will not kill a healthy hen. What ends her is the coop. The winter that decides whether your flock thrives starts the day you move them inside. It is the litter, the air, the light, and the water.

## Why Winter Matters for Laying Hens

The morning looks calm. Hens roost high and dry while frost spreads across the roof. The cold outside is survivable when the coop is right. Illinois Extension notes this, and Ohio State backs it up. Hens adjust to the cold over time. Acclimation is the point. Give them weeks to settle and they handle a freeze that catches a fresh flock off guard.

Ohio State says supplemental heat is usually unnecessary when the coop is dry, draught-free, and well-ventilated. Cold-tolerant breeds to watch for include Ameraucanas, Brahma, Cochins, Rhode Island Reds, and Wyandottes. Even so, large combs and wattles stay vulnerable.

Moisture is the real enemy. A coop that stays damp raises frostbite risk. Breed choice matters less than keeping the litter dry.

## Heating and Air Quality

The instinct to heat a coop is where winter care goes wrong. Ohio State says unvented heaters and stoves are a hazard. A closed space filled with birds will not exhaust a stove or a space heater. Take care with heat lamps too. They are a fire risk near bedding and cords the chickens can reach.

Ohio State says acclimated, cold-tolerant birds in a dry, ventilated coop do not usually need supplemental heat. Keep the heat source off the bedding. If you do heat, choose a device that vents to outside, and never put a heat lamp where loose litter can catch fire.

Ventilation keeps the ammonia down. Ohio State says ventilation must stay open even in cold weather. A coop sealed tight to hold warmth builds carbon dioxide and ammonia the birds breathe in, and it traps the moisture that causes frostbite. Airflow above the birds, still air at the roost, is the aim.

## Housing: The Winter Coop Checklist

Three rules. Dry, draught-free, well-ventilated. The wording comes from Illinois Extension. The floor should carry a 6 to 12 inch layer of dry bedding, whether wood shavings, straw, or hay. It insulates the floor and keeps the feet off the cold.

Roost sizing is a quick number. Give 6 to 8 inches of roost space per bird. That lets hens climb out of the cold and out of the draught.

## Ventilation vs. Drafts: Getting It Right

Keep the air moving so ammonia and moisture never pool, then block the draughts that reach the birds. Both Illinois Extension and Ohio State say the same thing. The instinct to seal the coop tight in winter is wrong. Closing all vents traps warmth and traps the very moisture that causes frostbite and respiratory trouble.

Concrete airflow methods help. Roof or eave vents move air without touching the birds. A partially opened window does the same.

Tie ventilation back to the health that matters. Trapped humidity feeds both frostbite and respiratory problems. Ammonia builds where air cannot escape.

## Lighting for Egg Production

Winter days fall short of what laying hens need. They want 14 to 16 hours of light per day to keep producing eggs. Ohio State says hens require 14 hours and recommends 12 to 14. The sun simply will not deliver it in the short days.

The setup is a matter of maths. A 60-watt incandescent bulb or an 800-lumen LED at 7 feet, with a reflector, covers roughly 200 square feet. Mount it in the coop area to reach the whole floor.

Add the light with a timer. The timer adds hours steadily, so the flock wakes and starts laying on a steady rhythm. Kansas State points to the timer as the practical tool for winter egg production.

## Water: Keeping It Liquid and Safe

The rule is simple to state and hard to hold. Water must be provided at least every 10 hours, day and night. Illinois Extension and Kansas State both give the 10-hour mark. Pair it with a heated waterer or a well-insulated container.

Open water containers indoors also add humidity, so keep them away from the living space. The Virginia Cooperative Extension flags open basins as a moisture problem.

The electrical side matters too. Keep cords off the floor. Keep flammable material away from any heat source near the waterer. A heat lamp and a bedding pile are not a good combination.

## Feeding for Warmth and Productivity

Hens eat more in cold weather. That is the first fact. They need the energy to stay warm. Offer free-choice feed, or scatter high-energy grains like oats and corn in the evening. Illinois Extension and Kansas State recommend the evening scatter.

Feed the layer ration first, then let scratch grain be the treat. Scratch grain adds calories but lacks the protein and calcium hens need for eggs. Two sources flag this. It should never replace layer feed.

Layer feed should be 14 to 17 percent crude protein. That range suits a laying bird.

## Frostbite: Prevention and First Aid

The usual victims are the combs, the wattles, and the feet. Wet, humid conditions push the risk up. Prevention is a routine. Clear snow from the runs. Size the roosts at 6 to 8 inches per bird. Apply petroleum jelly to large combs and wattles in extreme cold. Illinois Extension and Ohio State lay out these three steps.

Once frostbite appears, look for the tell-tale signs. Gray, black, or brittle areas indicate it. Dead tissue is not saved. Warm the affected part slowly in a warm space, never with rapid warming. Warm foot soaks help, but never soak the comb or wattle. If infection is suspected, steer the flock toward a qualified vet.

## Predator Proofing in Cold Weather

Foxes, raccoons, weasels, and birds of prey are a constant threat. The Virginia Cooperative Extension says the fix is buried fencing.

Bury the wire 12 inches deep. Toe it outward 6 inches to stop digging predators from getting under. That defeats the common attack.

Cover the runs with mesh wire or netting to hold off hawks and owls. Lock the birds up before dark with a latched door.

## Daily and Weekly Winter Chores

The daily rhythm is short. Collect eggs once or twice a day to stop them freezing. Discard any egg that cracks or has frozen. Sweep spilled water and wet litter to hold humidity down. Ohio State and Kansas State both name the twice-daily egg check.

The weekly checks are a list. Inspect bedding depth and replace matted layers. Test the waterer and the timer. Look for frostbite signs across the flock. Illinois Extension rounds out the list with the bedding and litter check.

A clean, dry coop does the work of any heat. Routine beats rescue once the first frost arrives.

## Safety

Heating a coop means managing fire and air hazards directly. Heat lamps near loose bedding or dry litter are a fire risk. Keep cords off the floor, away from anything the chickens can peck or pull. Do not leave unvented heaters or stoves in the coop, since the carbon dioxide and fumes pile back into the air the birds breathe.

Poor ventilation lets ammonia and carbon dioxide build to levels that harm the respiratory system. Keep continuous airflow even in freezing weather. A coop sealed tight to hold warmth does the opposite. Wet litter and open water containers add humidity, which raises frostbite risk. Remove wet litter daily, and keep water containers away from the roosts.

Ohio State says acclimated, cold-tolerant birds in a dry, ventilated coop do not usually need supplemental heat. Overheating can be more harmful than the cold itself.
