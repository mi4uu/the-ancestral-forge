The salt is the first thing that decides whether a batch ferments or spoils. The extension services are firm about the number, and the reason is that too little salt lets spoilage bacteria win while the good bacteria starve. One university handout puts it plainly: the salt level in a fermented vegetable should not be lowered, and salt substitutes cannot be used in place of salt.

## Why salt amount matters

Too little salt lets the bad bacteria take over. The good bacteria need the salt to get started, and then the cabbage makes its own acid to finish the job. The acid is the preserving part. It drops the pH and holds the spoilage bacteria at bay.

The salt buys the cabbage time to make that acid. It gets the cabbage to the point where the acid can do its work without letting the bad bacteria win along the way. That is why the extension services tie salt so tightly to the weight of the cabbage.

## The weight-based percentage and how to measure it

One university food-preservation handout gives the salt amount as a percentage. It says to use 2.25 to 2.50 percent salt by weight of cabbage. That is a mass-for-mass figure. Weigh the cabbage, weigh the salt, and the number means something.

Two and a quarter percent of one hundred pounds is two and a quarter pounds of salt. That is the arithmetic behind the percentage. The number is a mass-for-mass figure, not a volume measure.

Flake salt varies in density. So does the salt in a shaker. A cup of one is not a cup of another. Converting a volume measure to a percentage without weighing is a guess. Weigh the salt instead. The number then holds.

The handout also warns that flake salt packs differently than fine salt, so a cup-for-cup swap between salts changes the amount. Stick to one type and weigh it, and the percentage stays true across batches. A scale is the honest measure. It reads the mass directly, and it does not care whether the salt is fine or flaky.

## Recipe-volume ratios versus salt percentage

Most recipes do not give a percentage. They give a volume ratio. A tested recipe uses three tablespoons of canning or pickling salt per five pounds of cabbage. Another university source gives the same batch ratio. Three tablespoons of salt per five pounds of shredded cabbage.

The tested recipe names canning or pickling salt specifically. That is a detail worth keeping, since other salts carry additives that change the ferment. The volume ratio and the percentage point the same direction, but they are not the same thing. The volume ratio is a starting point. Weighing the salt keeps the batch true across batches.

Work the ratio out before you weigh. Three tablespoons per five pounds is one and a half tablespoons per half a batch, and six tablespoons per ten pounds. The ratio scales without a calculator, but a scale still settles the final number.

If you weigh a batch, the percentage is the check. Ten pounds of cabbage at 2.5 percent is a quarter pound of salt. That is the same ballpark as the volume ratio, and now you have two ways to confirm the amount. The two figures should come close, but the percentage is the tighter standard. The volume ratio is a rough guide.

## Packing, brine, and keeping cabbage submerged

The cabbage must sit under liquid. Press it until its own juice covers it. If the juice is not enough, add boiled-and-cooled brine. That brine uses one and a half tablespoons of salt per quart of water.

Boil the water, cool it, then dissolve the salt. Add the brine to the cabbage only after it has cooled. Hot brine cooks the cabbage. The finished brine is a top-up, not the whole bath. Most of the cabbage sits in its own juice.

One university source recommends keeping the cabbage under one to two inches of brine. A plate and a weight hold it down. A food-grade brine-filled bag works too. The cabbage has to stay under the surface. Air exposure is the risk here.

## Temperature and fermentation timeline

Temperature decides the pace. One centre recommends fermenting at 70 to 75°F for about three to four weeks. Cool it to 60 to 65°F and the batch takes about five to six weeks.

Below 60°F the cabbage may not ferment. Above 75°F it may soften. The window is narrow. Pick a spot that stays steady. A place that swings between cold and warm will not give the cabbage a chance to work.

## Normal surface changes versus spoilage signs

Check the kraut two to three times a week when you use a plate or a jar weight. Remove surface scum if it forms. Scum on top is a surface thing. It is a top layer, and it is not proof the whole batch is bad.

A finished batch should be firm, with clear brine. A bad odor, mould, or slimy texture are reasons not to taste it. When those signs show up, the batch goes. No tasting.

## Food-safe vessels, sodium, and when to discard a batch

Use glass or heavy food-grade plastic. Avoid metal. Avoid non-food-grade plastic. Old, cracked, or chipped ceramic crocks may leach lead or other metals. A crack or chip is a place for metal to work its way into the food. Keep the vessel clean and whole.

Sauerkraut is salty. Account for its salt content in meals. Rinsing finished kraut helps if it is too salty for the dish.

## Safety

Do not cut salt to make the kraut less salty. Too little salt can cause spoilage rather than fermentation. If the kraut is too salty, rinse it before eating. Don't cut the salt.

Do not taste suspect kraut. Mould, slime, and a bad odor are reasons to keep your hands off the batch.

Keep the cabbage submerged and limit air exposure. Air exposure is the risk here.

Use suitable food-contact equipment. Damaged ceramic crocks may leach lead or other metals into the food. Glass and heavy food-grade plastic are the safe choices.

Sauerkraut is salty. People who must restrict sodium should account for its salt content in their meals.
