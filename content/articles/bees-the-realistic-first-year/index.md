U.S. beekeepers lost 39.9% of their colonies between April 2025 and April 2026. Backyard keepers with fewer than 50 hives lost more than commercial operators.

## The First-Year Reality Check

First-year beekeeping is about learning the rhythm of the hive. University of Georgia Extension says first-year colonies rarely produce surplus honey. The focus is on building comb, population and winter stores.

University of Missouri Extension recommends starting with 2–5 hives to absorb losses and learn the craft before expanding. UGA Extension advises one or two, no more. The record is clear on this: more hives give you more margin for error, but a single hive can still work if you are diligent.

## Costs: What You’ll Spend Before the Bees Arrive

A full Langstroth hive setup includes a bottom board, boxes, frames, foundation, an inner cover and a telescoping cover. Add a smoker, hive tool, bee brush and a veil or full suit. Many beginners underestimate the cost of quality protective gear, but skimp here and you risk stings, or worse, an allergic reaction.

Bees come as nucleus colonies for $150–$200 or packages for $100–$125. Primal Bee notes that operating costs for a hive typically run about $200 to $400 per year, and advises readers to get a current local quote rather than budget from a published number.

## Time Commitment: Inspections, Feeding, and Learning

Neglect leads to collapse. Inspections check for disease, queen status and food stores. Feeding schedules and mite checks are part of the routine. Beekeeping demands consistent attention, especially in the first year when colonies are most vulnerable.

You are looking for signs of a healthy queen: eggs, larvae and capped brood in a tight pattern. Feeding is another time commitment. A new colony often needs supplemental sugar syrup to build comb and stores, especially if it started as a package. Mixing syrup, filling feeders and monitoring consumption adds to your weekly routine.

## Losses: Why Colonies Die and How to Prevent It

Varroa mites weaken bees by feeding on them and spreading viruses. UGA Extension says regular monitoring and treatment are essential for first-year survival. Untreated infestations can kill a colony in months.

Colony Collapse Disorder involves the sudden disappearance of worker bees, leaving the queen and brood behind. The EPA links CCD to Varroa, pesticides, poor nutrition and stress. Piles of dead bees near the hive indicate a die-off is not CCD.

Starvation often happens when bees run out of stored honey, especially in late winter. Supplemental feeding with sugar syrup or fondant can prevent this.

## Local Rules: Permits, Zoning, and Neighbour Relations

California’s BeeWhere system charges $10 for 1–9 hives, $100 for 10–50 and $250 for 51 or more, according to the California Master Beekeeper Program. These rules vary by locality, so checking with local authorities is essential before setting up hives.

Registration helps officials track hive locations in case of disease outbreaks. Setback requirements are designed to minimise conflicts with neighbours, but they can be a challenge in urban or suburban areas. If your property is small, you may need to negotiate with neighbours or seek a variance.

Neighbour relations are critical. Even if your locality does not require notification, it is good practice to inform neighbours about your plans. Be prepared to address concerns about stings, noise or property values.

## Varroa Mites and Other Threats: A Beginner’s Defence Plan

Varroa mites are the most persistent threat to new colonies. UGA Extension emphasises that regular monitoring and treatment are essential for first-year survival. Mite checks should be part of every inspection, and treatments should be applied as needed.

Other threats include pests and diseases that can weaken or destroy a colony. Good hive management, regular inspections and prompt action when problems are detected can keep these threats in check.

## What Success Looks Like: No Honey, Just Survival

Success in the first year is measured by strong comb, a healthy population and enough winter stores. UGA Extension and Primal Bee both warn that harvesting honey in Year 1 risks starvation. The goal is to get the colony through the winter.

A successful first year ends with a colony that is active and healthy. If you achieve this, you have laid the foundation for a productive apiary in Year 2.

## Hidden Costs and Common Mistakes to Avoid

First-year expenses do not end with the initial setup. Primal Bee cites operating costs of about $200 to $400 per hive per year. Cheap or used gear may carry pests or fail, costing more in replacements and lost colonies. Sanitising equipment from deadouts is critical to prevent disease spread.

Common mistakes include underfeeding, neglecting mite treatments and failing to monitor hive health. Many beginners assume that bees can fend for themselves, but first-year colonies need active management. Another mistake is using old or contaminated equipment. Always clean and sterilise used hive components, and avoid buying secondhand gear unless you are certain it is pest-free. Never combine collapsing colonies with healthy ones.

## Year Two and Beyond: When the Investment Pays Off

If Year 1 goals are met, Year 2 brings the possibility of honey harvests and colony splits. The investment of time and money in the first year sets the stage for a productive apiary in the years to come.

## Safety

Varroa mites weaken colonies by feeding on bees and spreading viruses. Untreated infestations can kill a colony in months. Colony Collapse Disorder involves the sudden disappearance of worker bees, leaving the queen and brood behind. It is linked to Varroa, pesticides, poor nutrition and stress. Piles of dead bees near the hive indicate a die-off is not CCD. Protective gear such as a veil or suit is essential for beginners. EpiPens may be required for allergic individuals. Never combine collapsing colonies with healthy ones. Sanitise equipment from deadouts to prevent contamination.
